# Lecture 4 · Sets and Quantifiers

## Key points

A set collects objects into one whole; a [quantifier](concept:quantifier) says whether a statement holds for every object in the set or for at least one. "Every square is a rectangle" and "some rectangles are squares" talk about the same shapes, yet they are different propositions.

## Explanation

### Sets and elements

A set is determined by its elements. \(x \in A\) reads "x belongs to A". Let A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} be the faces of a die and B = {2, 4, 6} its even numbers. Every element of B is in A, so B is a subset of A, written \(B \subseteq A\). The elements two sets share form the intersection \(B \cap C\); everything in either forms the union \(B \cup C\).

The nested boxes below show subsets: every square is a rectangle and every rectangle is a quadrilateral, but not the other way round.

### Universal and existential quantifiers

- **Universal** \(\forall\) ("every", "all"): \(\forall x \in A,\ P(x)\) means every element of A satisfies P.
- **Existential** \(\exists\) ("there is", "at least one"): \(\exists x \in A,\ P(x)\) means at least one element of A satisfies P.

"Every square is a rectangle" can be rewritten as: for any shape x, if x is a square, then x is a rectangle. A universal statement often hides the "if ... then ..." of Lecture 2.

### Negation flips the quantifier

The negation of "every reading is within the error range" is not "no reading is", but "at least one reading is not":

$$
\neg\,(\forall x,\ P(x)) \iff \exists x,\ \neg P(x) \qquad \neg\,(\exists x,\ P(x)) \iff \forall x,\ \neg P(x)
$$

This is why a single [counterexample](concept:counterexample) refutes a universal statement.

## Example

[](fig:log-venn) draws the die faces A as a box with two subsets: the even numbers B = {2, 4, 6} and the numbers greater than 4, C = {5, 6}. The circles overlap only at 6, so \(B \cap C = \lbrace 6 \rbrace\) and \(B \cup C = \lbrace 2, 4, 5, 6 \rbrace\); 1 and 3 lie outside both.

| Statement | Written as | True? | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every number in A is less than 7 | \(\forall x \in A,\ x < 7\) | Yes | All six checked |
| Every number in A is even | \(\forall x \in A,\ 2 \mid x\) | No | Counterexample: 1 |
| Some number in A is greater than 5 | \(\exists x \in A,\ x > 5\) | Yes | Example: 6 |
| Some number in A is greater than 6 | \(\exists x \in A,\ x > 6\) | No | None of the six is |

## Common mistakes

- **Negating "all" as "none"**: "not every number is even" means "some number is odd".
- **Reading "some" as "only some"**: in mathematics "there is" asks for at least one; all of them is fine too.
- **Forgetting the domain**: "every number is greater than 0" is true in A and false among the integers.

:::: details Self-check

**Question 1**　Write the negation of "every measurement is within the error range".

::: details Answer
At least one measurement is not within the error range.
:::

**Question 2**　In A = {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6}, is \(\exists x \in A,\ x^2 = 10\) true or false?

::: details Answer
False. The squares of 1 to 6 are 1, 4, 9, 16, 25, 36; none of them is 10.
:::

**Question 3**　Is "every even number is a multiple of 4" true or false? If it is false, give a counterexample.

::: details Answer
False. A counterexample is 2 (6 or 10 also work): it is even, but not a multiple of 4.
:::

## Summary

- A set is determined by its elements; a subset's elements all belong to the larger set.
- \(\forall\) means "every", \(\exists\) means "there is"; a universal statement is often an "if ... then ...".
- Negation flips the quantifier: "not every" equals "there is one that does not".
